About This Manual

MacMusic turns your Macintosh Plus into a music player. It keeps your songs in a library on your hard disk, plays them through the Macintosh’s own speaker, and lets you find and play them with a click wheel and a small screen, as you would a pocket music player. This manual shows you how.

What you need

To use MacMusic, you need:

  • a Macintosh Plus with System 6.0.7;
  • an Apple Hard Disk 20SC or other SCSI hard disk, for MacMusic and your library;
  • under MultiFinder, 640K of memory for MacMusic (it works in 512K);
  • your music, as MacMusic’s kind of sound file (see below).

Other MacMusic manuals

MacMusic Utilities, a separate manual, explains how to make your music into MacMusic’s sound files and bring them to your Macintosh. This manual names it wherever you’ll need it.

What you should know

This manual assumes you know the basics of using your Macintosh: pointing, clicking and dragging with the mouse, choosing commands from menus, working with windows, and using disks. If you don’t, start with the Macintosh Owner’s Guide and its guided tour.

How to use this manual

There are three ways in:

  • Learn it: Chapter 1, “Learning MacMusic,” takes you from installing MacMusic to making your first playlist, in ten short lessons.
  • Look it up: Chapter 2, “Using MacMusic,” shows how to do each task, one to a column. Chapter 3, “MacMusic Reference,” describes every screen, menu and command, and the appendixes list MacMusic’s messages and what to do when something goes wrong.
  • Just try it: nothing you do on MacMusic’s screen can harm your songs, and MENU always takes you back.

The glossary explains the words this manual uses, and the index finds any subject.

If you’ve used a pocket music player

Much will feel familiar: the click wheel, the menus, Now Playing, playlists, Shuffle and Repeat all work as you’d expect. A few things are different:

  • You turn the click wheel with the mouse, not your thumb, and the keyboard works too.
  • Your library is a folder on your hard disk. You add songs by importing them, with the File menu.
  • The music comes from the Macintosh’s own sound: mono, through its speaker or its sound output port.
  • There’s no battery to run down; the battery on the screen is just for show.

What Is MacMusic?

MacMusic turns your Macintosh into a music player. It plays songs stored on your disk through the Macintosh’s own speaker, and you work it much as you would a pocket music player: by turning a wheel and clicking buttons, all with the mouse.

Before you start the lessons, it helps to know the three ideas MacMusic is built on. None of them is new; you probably have all three at home already.

Your record shelf

MacMusic keeps every song in one place, a folder on your hard disk called the MacMusic Library. Like a shelf of records, it’s where your music lives. You put a song in it by importing it, and MacMusic looks after it from then on: it files the song under a number of its own, and keeps its title, artist and album with it.

A library can hold as many as 500 songs, if your disk has room for them.

A mix tape

A playlist is like a mix tape you’ve made for a long drive: a few favourite songs, in the order you want to hear them. Unlike a tape, though, a playlist doesn’t copy anything. The songs stay where they are in the library, and the playlist simply lists them. A song can be on several playlists at once, and taking it off one doesn’t remove it from your library.

You can have as many as ten playlists.

A pocket music player

The MacMusic window looks like a pocket music player, and it works like one. At the top is MacMusic’s screen, where you see lists of your music and what’s playing. Below it is the click wheel: you turn it to move through a list, and click it to choose. Everything you do to play music happens in this window.

Everything you do to look after your music—importing songs, making playlists, choosing a library—happens in the usual Macintosh way, with commands in the menu bar at the top of the screen.

What you’ll learn

The lessons that follow take about half an hour. You’ll install MacMusic on your hard disk, create a library, import the song that comes on the MacMusic disk, play it, and put it on a playlist of your own.

You should already know how to use your Macintosh: how to point, click and drag with the mouse, and how to choose commands from menus. If you don’t, spend some time with the Macintosh Owner’s Guide that came with your computer, and its guided tour disk first.

1Making a Backup Copy

Before you use MacMusic, make a copy of the MacMusic disk, and keep the original somewhere safe. Then, if anything happens to the disk you use, you can always make another copy.

Lock the MacMusic disk.

Turn the disk over and slide the small tab in its corner so that you can see through the hole. A locked disk can be read, but nothing on it can be changed or thrown away.

Copy the disk onto a blank 800K disk.

Insert the MacMusic disk, and then the blank disk (if the Finder asks, click Initialize to prepare the blank disk first). Drag the MacMusic disk’s icon onto the blank disk’s icon. The Finder copies everything on it, asking you to swap the two disks when it needs to; follow its messages.

If you’re not sure how to initialise or copy a disk, see the Macintosh Owner’s Guide.

Put the original MacMusic disk away, and use the copy for the rest of the lessons.

2Installing MacMusic

MacMusic runs from your hard disk: an Apple Hard Disk 20SC or other SCSI hard disk. Your library goes on the hard disk too, as songs take far more room than a floppy disk can hold.

The MacMusic disk holds three things: the MacMusic application; Song.wav, a short song for the lessons (half a minute of “Chariots of Fire” by Vangelis); and MacMusic Read Me, which you can open with TeachText.

Insert your copy of the MacMusic disk, and open it.

Drag the MacMusic icon and the Song.wav icon to your hard disk’s window.

To drag both at once, click one, then hold down the Shift key and click the other; then drag either of them. The Finder copies them to the hard disk.

You can put MacMusic in any folder you like. For the lessons, leave both icons in the hard disk’s window, where you’ll find them easily.

Your hard disk’s name and window will look different from the picture.

Eject the MacMusic disk.

Choose Eject from the File menu. You won’t need the disk again in these lessons.

3Starting MacMusic

Double-click the MacMusic icon on your hard disk.

The MacMusic window opens, and the Macintosh menu bar shows MacMusic’s menus: File, Edit and Playlists. For a moment the Apple logo appears on MacMusic’s screen, as on a music player starting up.

Under MultiFinder, MacMusic starts the same way. It needs 512K of memory, and works best with 640K.

4Creating Your MacMusic Library

The first time you start MacMusic, it doesn’t know where your music is, so it asks:

Create New makes a new, empty library. Find Existing opens a library you already have, on this disk or another. Not Now carries on with no library at all.

Click Create New.

A dialog box asks where to put the library, and what to call it.

Make sure your hard disk’s name is shown above the list, and click Save.

If another disk’s name is shown, click Drive until your hard disk’s name appears. The name MacMusic suggests, “MacMusic Library,” is a good one; you can type another if you like.

MacMusic makes the library folder, and its screen shows the main menu. Your library is ready, but empty.

MacMusic remembers your library, and opens it every time it starts.

If you look in your hard disk’s window in the Finder later, you’ll see the new folder. Inside it are a Playlists folder, a file called MacMusic Library Info, which tells MacMusic that the folder is a library, and, once you’ve imported some songs, the songs themselves:

Important

Leave the files in your MacMusic Library to MacMusic. Don’t rename, move or add files inside the library with the Finder; use MacMusic’s commands, and your library will always be in order.

5The MacMusic Window

Take a moment to look at the MacMusic window.

You’ll use the click wheel for almost everything you do in the window:

  • Turn the wheel to move the highlight up and down a list.
  • Click the centre button to choose the highlighted item.
  • Click MENU, at the top of the wheel, to go back to the screen before.
  • Click the bottom of the wheel to play or pause, and the left and right sides to go to the previous or next song.

The window’s title bar and close box work like any other window’s: drag the title bar to move the window, and click the close box to quit MacMusic.

6Using the Click Wheel

The main menu lists the things you can do: Music, Extras, Settings, Shuffle Songs and Backlight. A > at the right of an item means it leads to another list.

To turn the click wheel: Position the pointer on the wheel, hold down the mouse button, and move the pointer round the wheel, as though you were turning it with your finger.

Turn the click wheel clockwise, slowly.

The highlight moves down the list, one item for every short turn you make. Turn it anticlockwise, and the highlight moves up.

Turn the wheel until Music is highlighted, and click the centre button.

The Music screen appears. It sorts your music in several ways: by playlist, artist, album, song title, genre and composer, and it keeps audiobooks apart. The list is longer than the screen; the bar at the right shows that there’s more below, and turning the wheel scrolls to it.

Click MENU, at the top of the wheel.

You’re back at the main menu. MENU always takes you back one screen, so you can never get lost.

You can also use the keyboard: the Up Arrow and Down Arrow keys move the highlight, Return chooses, and Delete goes back.

Your library is still empty, so there’s nothing to play yet. The next lesson puts a song in it.

7Importing a Song

Choose Import Song from the File menu.

The commands in the File menu, and in the Edit and Playlists menus beside it, look after your library. The Import Song dialog box appears, listing the files on your hard disk.

Select Song.wav and click Open.

MacMusic tells you what it’s about to do:

Because Song.wav is on the same disk as your library, MacMusic moves it into the library, which is quick. (From a floppy disk or another hard disk, MacMusic copies the song and then removes the original from that disk, unless you choose Just Copy. Chapter 2 explains.)

Click OK.

A dialog box shows the song’s details: its file name and length at the top, then its title, artist and so on. MacMusic has filled in the title from the file’s name. You can do better.

Type the song’s title:

Chariots of Fire

The title is already selected, so what you type replaces it.

Press the Tab key, and type the artist’s name:

Press Tab again, and type the album’s name:

Chariots of Fire

Your own music has to be made into WAV files before MacMusic can play it. MacMusic Utilities, a separate manual, explains how.

Click Import.

For a moment you see “Importing…”, and the song is in your library. MacMusic asks whether you’d like to import another song; with more to add, you’d click Import Another and choose the next one (Chapter 2 explains).

Click Done.

MacMusic reads your library again, saying “Loading the library…”, and shows its main menu.

8Playing a Song

Turn the wheel to highlight Music, and click the centre button. Then highlight Songs, and click the centre button again.

The Songs screen lists every song in your library by title, in alphabetical order. For now there’s just one, “Chariots of Fire.”

With “Chariots of Fire” highlighted, click the centre button.

Click the bottom of the click wheel.

The Space bar pauses and plays, too.

Turn the click wheel a little, either way.

Type a name for the playlist, and click Create.

10Quitting

Choose Quit from the File menu.

The music stops, the MacMusic window closes, and you’re back in the Finder.

You can also quit by pressing Command-Q, or by clicking the close box in the MacMusic window.

What Now?

You’ve learned the basics: you’ve set up MacMusic, imported a song, played it and made a playlist. That’s most of what you’ll ever need.

When you’ve imported more of your music, you might like to try:

  • Shuffle Songs, in the main menu, which plays every song in your library in a random order;
  • Artists and Albums, in the Music screen, which find your music the way you remember it;
  • Settings, where Shuffle and Repeat change how lists play;
  • Extras, where the Clock keeps you company.

Chapter 2, “Using MacMusic,” shows how to do each of these, and everything else MacMusic does, one task at a time. Chapter 3, “MacMusic Reference,” describes every menu, command and screen.

Or simply explore. Nothing you do on MacMusic’s screen can harm your songs, and MENU always brings you back.

Finding Music

Everything in your library can be found from the Music screen. Choose Music from the main menu, and you can look by playlist, artist, album, song title, genre or composer, with audiobooks kept apart.

  • Go Back
  • Find a Song by Its Title
  • Find an Artist’s Songs
  • Find an Album
  • Use “All”
  • Find Songs by Genre or Composer

Your music is sorted by the details you give each song. The more you fill in, the more ways you can find it.

To Scroll a List

Turn the click wheel: clockwise to move down the list, anticlockwise to move up.

When a list is longer than the screen, a scroll bar appears at its right, and the list scrolls as the highlight reaches the top or the bottom.

OR

Press the Down Arrow or Up Arrow key.

To Go Back

Click MENU, at the top of the click wheel.

You go back one screen, to the list you came from, with the same item highlighted.

OR

Press the Delete key.

On the main menu, MENU does nothing: there’s nowhere further back to go.

To Find a Song by Its Title

Choose Songs from the Music screen.

Every song in your library is listed in alphabetical order of title. A leading “The” is ignored, so “The Long Way Round” is listed under L.

Turn the click wheel to the song, and click the centre button to play it.

Audiobooks have a list of their own, and aren’t listed here.

To Find an Artist’s Songs

Choose Artists from the Music screen.

Choose an artist.

You see the artist’s albums.

Choose an album to see its songs, in track order, or choose All to see every song by that artist.

To Find an Album

Choose Albums from the Music screen.

Every album in your library is listed in alphabetical order.

Choose an album.

Its songs are listed in track order, as on the record.

To Use “All”

The Artists, Albums and Genres lists each begin with All.

Choose All at the top of the list.

All gathers up everything the list leads to: All in Genres lists every artist; All in Artists, every album; and All in an artist’s albums, every song by that artist, album by album, each in track order. All in Albums lists every song the same way.

The Composers list has no All.

To Find Songs by Genre or Composer

Choose Genres from the Music screen, and then a genre.

You see the artists with songs in that genre; choose one for their albums, and then the songs.

Or choose Composers, and then a composer.

That composer’s songs are listed straight away.

Shuffling

Shuffle plays songs in a random order, like a radio station that only plays your records.

  • Shuffle Every Song
  • Shuffle a List
  • Shuffle by Albums

Audiobooks are never shuffled.

To Shuffle Every Song

Choose Shuffle Songs from the main menu.

Every song in your library plays, in a random order, and Now Playing appears.

To Shuffle a List

Choose Settings from the main menu.

Highlight Shuffle, and click the centre button until it says Songs.

Each click shows the next setting: Off, Songs, Albums.

Play a list.

The song you chose plays first; the rest of the list follows in a random order.

The setting stays until you change it.

In Settings, set Shuffle to Albums.

Play a list.

Its albums play in a random order, each one’s songs in track order, as their records would.

Moving Through a Song

While a song plays, the Now Playing screen lets you skip ahead, go back, or jump to any place in it, and set how loud it is.

  • Fast-Forward or Rewind
  • Use the Scrubber
  • Change the Volume

The music keeps playing while you do any of these.

To Fast-Forward or Rewind

Position the pointer on the right side of the click wheel and hold down the mouse button. To rewind, use the left side.

After half a second the play icon changes to two arrows, and the song moves on at four times its normal speed; after two seconds, sixteen times.

Release the mouse button.

The song carries on from the new place.

OR

Hold down Option and the Right Arrow or Left Arrow key.

To Use the Scrubber

On Now Playing, click the centre button.

A diamond, the scrubber, appears on the progress bar.

Turn the click wheel.

One full turn moves through the whole song. When you stop turning, the song jumps to the new place.

The scrubber goes away by itself after five seconds.

To Change the Volume

On Now Playing, turn the click wheel: clockwise for louder, anticlockwise for softer.

The volume bar takes the place of the progress bar. One full turn goes from the softest setting to the loudest.

OR

Press the Down Arrow key for louder, the Up Arrow key for softer, one step at a time.

MacMusic remembers the volume for next time.

Headphones, Speakers and Tape

The sound output port on the back of your Macintosh lets you listen through headphones, play your music through a speaker or a stereo, or record it onto tape.

  • Listen Through Headphones
  • Play Through a Speaker or Stereo
  • Record Onto Tape

The Macintosh has one sound channel, so its sound, and MacMusic’s, is mono. While something is plugged into the port, the built-in speaker is silent. For more about the port, see the Macintosh Owner’s Guide.

To Listen Through Headphones

On Now Playing, turn the click wheel anticlockwise to the softest volume.

Plug the headphones into the sound output port, and put them on.

Play a song, and turn the click wheel clockwise, a step at a time, to a comfortable level.

The port takes a mono 3.5 mm plug. Stereo headphones need an adaptor to hear the music in both ears.

To Play Through a Speaker or Stereo

Use an amplified speaker, or a stereo’s auxiliary or line input; not a microphone or record-player input.

Turn the speaker’s volume all the way down, and turn MacMusic’s down too.

Connect the cable: a mono 3.5 mm plug at the Macintosh, and an adaptor if the inputs are stereo, so the sound comes from both sides.

Play a song. Bring up the speaker’s volume to a comfortable level, then set MacMusic’s to suit.

Elements of MacMusic

MacMusic works with three things: a library, the songs in it, and playlists. This section describes each, and its limits.

The MacMusic Library

A MacMusic Library is a folder, on any disk, that holds your songs and playlists. MacMusic uses one library at a time; you can have as many as you like, and switch between them with the File menu. Inside the folder are:

  • MacMusic Library Info, a small file that marks the folder as a library. To open a library, you open this file.
  • Playlists, a folder holding a text file for each playlist.
  • a song file for each song, named by its number.

Each song is given a number of its own when it’s imported. A number is never used twice in the same library, even after its song has been exported.

MacMusic reads the whole library each time it starts, and when you choose Rescan Library. For a library of 500 songs, that takes between a minute and a half and two minutes.

Only MacMusic should change a library’s files. A song file copied in from another library may have the same number as one already there; two songs with the same number can’t both be used, and MacMusic tells you which one it has left out.

MacMusic keeps its preferences (the library in use, the libraries you’ve used before, the volume and the settings) in a file called MacMusic Prefs, in the System Folder.

Songs

A song file is the WAV file you imported, unchanged, with MacMusic’s details for it added at the end. That’s why importing can simply move a file into the library, and why exporting gives back exactly the file you imported. Each song has:

  • a title, which every song must have;
  • an artist, an album, a genre and a composer, any of which can be left empty;
  • a track number, from 1 to 9,999, or none;
  • the Audiobook box, checked or not;
  • a rating: none, or one to five stars.

The title and the other details can be up to 63 characters each. An audiobook is a song with its Audiobook box checked: it’s listed only under Audiobooks, and never shuffled.

Playlists

A playlist is a text file in the library’s Playlists folder, named after the playlist. Each line lists one song, by its number and, for anyone reading the file, its title; MacMusic reads only the number. A song can be on a playlist more than once. Exporting a song takes it off every playlist.

  • A library can have as many as 10 playlists.
  • A playlist’s name can be up to 31 characters, and can’t contain a colon (:).
  • MacMusic reads as many as 1,000 songs from one playlist.

Messages saying the WAV file is damaged or incomplete, has no format information or audio data, or that its format information is inconsistent

The file is damaged, or wasn’t finished when it was made. Make a new copy from the original.

“The file could not be opened.”, “The file could not be found.” and “The WAV file could not be read.”

The file was removed, or its disk ejected, or the disk is damaged. Check the disk, and try again.

“This file is already inside the MacMusic Library folder.”

It’s already one of your songs. Choose a file from outside the library.

“The MacMusic Library is full (500 songs). Export a song to make room.”

Export a song you no longer want, or start another library. If you were importing several songs, the import ends; those already imported stay.

“There is not enough memory to import a song.”

Quit other applications, or give MacMusic more memory.

“There is not enough space on the MacMusic Library’s disk for this song.”

Make room on the disk by throwing away files you don’t need, or by exporting songs; then import the song again.

“The song could not be imported.”

Something went wrong while the song was copied or moved; the number in brackets says what. The song isn’t in your library. Check the disks, and try again.

“The song was imported, but the original file couldn’t be removed from its disk, so it was left in place.”

The song is in your library. Its original is still on the other disk, probably because the disk is locked; throw it away yourself if you like.

“Please enter a title.” and “Track must be a whole number from 0 to 9999.”

In the song’s details, type a title (every song needs one), and a track number of 9,999 or less, or leave the track empty.

“The song details window could not be opened.”

There isn’t enough memory. Quit other applications, and try again.

“Choose a folder outside the MacMusic Library.”

An exported song can’t be saved inside the library. Choose another folder.

“That folder could not be found.” and “There is not enough space on that disk.”

Choose another folder or disk, or make room, and export again.

“The song could not be exported.”

The song is still in your library. Check the disk you chose, and try again.

The details weren’t changed, perhaps because the disk is locked or full.

“The song’s details were saved, but its title couldn’t be updated in every playlist.”

The song is changed. Playlists list songs by number, so they still work; only the title written in a playlist file for people to read is out of date.

Appendix B

About Sound Files

The Files MacMusic Plays

MacMusic plays WAV sound files of one kind:

  • mono: one channel of sound;
  • 22,254 or 22,050 samples a second;
  • 8 bits to each sample, uncompressed (“PCM”).

That’s the Macintosh Plus’s own sound. Its sound hardware plays 8-bit samples, about 22,000 a second, through one channel, to the built-in speaker or the sound output port. A file made for it plays just as it was made, with nothing lost along the way, and the Macintosh has time left over for everything else.

Because the Macintosh has one sound channel, MacMusic’s sound is mono, through the speaker and the sound output port alike. A stereo recording is mixed into one channel when its file is made.

The Macintosh Plus plays exactly 22,254 samples a second, and a file made at that rate, as the MacMusic Converter makes them, plays at its true speed and pitch. A file made at 22,050 samples a second, the rate of earlier versions of MacMusic, plays about 0.9% fast: a four-minute song ends two seconds early. Few ears can tell, and the times MacMusic shows are right either way.

How Much Room Music Takes

At about 22,000 bytes a second, music takes about 1.3 MB of disk space for each minute. So:

OnThere’s room for about
An 800K floppy disk30 seconds, beside MacMusic itself
A 20 MB hard disk, such as the Apple Hard Disk 20SC15 minutes, or four or five songs

A 2 GB hard disk, the largest System 6 can use25 hours

A full library of 500 songs, at four minutes a song, would need about 2.6 GB, more than one disk can hold; you can keep a library on each of several disks, and switch between them with the File menu.

Settings, About, and Library Info in the File menu both show how much room is left on the library’s disk.

Where Songs Come From

MacMusic plays the files; it doesn’t make them. Your music has to be prepared as MacMusic’s kind of WAV file, and brought to the Macintosh, before you can import it. MacMusic Utilities, a separate manual, explains how: see “Converting Songs.”

If a file isn’t the right kind, MacMusic says so when you import it, and says what the file is. The messages are listed in Appendix A, under “Importing Songs.”

Appendix D

Troubleshooting

If something isn’t working as you expect, look for it here. For a message MacMusic shows, see Appendix A.

No sound

Go to Now Playing and turn the click wheel clockwise: the volume may be turned down.

Check the sound output port on the back of the Macintosh. While anything is plugged into it, the built-in speaker is silent; if headphones or a speaker are connected, check them, and their own volume.

If MacMusic said “Sound playback isn’t available,” it can’t use this Macintosh’s sound; MacMusic is made for the Macintosh Plus.

MacMusic can’t find your library

Insert the library’s floppy disk, or make sure its hard disk is connected and switched on. Switch an external hard disk on before you switch on the Macintosh, so the Macintosh finds it as it starts up.

Then click Find Existing and open the library’s MacMusic Library Info file, or choose the library from the File menu.

A file won’t import

MacMusic says why; see Appendix A, “Importing Songs.” Most often the file isn’t MacMusic’s kind of WAV file. MacMusic Utilities explains how to prepare one.

The music pauses while another application works hard

MacMusic plays in the background, but if another application keeps the Macintosh completely busy for more than about six seconds (copying many files, say, or initialising a disk), the sound can run out. You may hear a moment repeat, then a pause; the music carries on by itself when the Macintosh is free.

Not enough memory under MultiFinder

MacMusic asks for 640K, and needs at least 512K. Quit applications you aren’t using, or give MacMusic less memory with Get Info (see Chapter 2).

Starting up takes a while

MacMusic reads your library each time it starts, and after you import songs, while the screen says Loading the library. It keeps a note of your songs, so on a Macintosh Plus this takes about 13 seconds for a library of 300 songs. The first time it opens a library, and when you choose Rescan Library, it reads every song file, which takes longer.

Song details look wrong after changing files in the Finder

MacMusic keeps a note of your songs, and reads a song again when its file changes. If a song was changed by another application and still shows its old details, choose Rescan Library, which reads every song.

The Macintosh forgets the time

If the date and time go back each time you switch on, the Macintosh’s clock battery is flat, and needs replacing. MacMusic copes with a clock that has stopped; after songs have been changed outside MacMusic, choose Rescan Library.

Nothing prints

Check that the printer is switched on, has paper, and is chosen in the Chooser; see Appendix A, “Printing.” If MacMusic says there isn’t enough memory, quit other applications, or give MacMusic more memory with Get Info (Chapter 2). Under MultiFinder with background printing on, a LaserWriter’s pages are printed by PrintMonitor once MacMusic has finished: leave the Macintosh switched on until they are.

Songs play a little fast

Songs made at 22,050 samples a second play about 0.9% fast, because the Macintosh Plus plays its sound a little faster than they were made for. The times shown are right. Songs made again with the MacMusic Converter play at their true speed. See Appendix B.

An earlier MacMusic leaves songs out

MacMusic 1.0 and 1.1 can play only songs made at 22,050 samples a second. Opening the library, they say that some files were not recognised, and leave those songs out. Nothing is changed: the songs are all there again when this version opens the library.

The softest volume isn’t silent

It’s the Macintosh Plus’s lowest sound level. To silence the music, pause it.

“A song’s file couldn’t be read”

The song’s file is damaged, or its disk has gone. Choose Rescan Library. If the song still won’t play, export it and import it again from the original file.

A song is missing from the lists

An audiobook is listed only under Audiobooks; check the song’s Audiobook box with Edit Song. If the song was copied into the library folder with the Finder, MacMusic may have skipped it: import songs with Import Song instead.

alert box

A box that appears on the screen to give a warning or a message, with a button, such as OK, to put it away.

A song marked as an audiobook in its details. Audiobooks are listed only under Audiobooks, and never shuffled.

In MacMusic, a novelty that turns MacMusic’s screen light on dark.

To pick a command from a menu, or an item on MacMusic’s screen.

The wheel below MacMusic’s screen. You turn it by dragging round it, and click its parts to choose, go back, play and skip.

The desk accessory, in the Apple menu, where you pick the printer: an ImageWriter, say, and the port it’s connected to.

In Extras: the time and date from the Macintosh’s own clock.

A small application, such as the Calculator or the Alarm Clock, chosen from the Apple menu.

A box in which MacMusic asks for information or a choice, such as a song’s details.

Shown in grey: a dimmed command or button can’t be used just now.

To position the pointer, hold down the mouse button, move the mouse, and release the button.

To give a song back from your library as a WAV file, which removes it from the library.

The application that shows your disks, folders and files, and lets you start applications.

A 3½-inch disk, such as the MacMusic disk, that holds 800K.

hard disk

A disk that holds far more than a floppy disk, inside or attached to the Macintosh: the place for MacMusic and your library.

highlight

To make an item stand out, white on black, to show that it’s the one a click will choose.

To keep a click wheel button pressed for half a second or more. On Now Playing, holding Next or Previous fast-forwards or rewinds.

To put a song into your library.

The MacMusic Library Info file, which marks a folder as a MacMusic Library.

The folder where MacMusic keeps your songs and playlists.

MacMusic’s window, with its screen and its click wheel.

The first list on MacMusic’s screen: Music, Extras, Settings, Shuffle Songs and Backlight.

The top of the click wheel. Clicking it goes back one screen.

menu bar

The strip across the top of the Macintosh screen holding the Apple, File, Edit and Playlists menus.

The part of System 6 that lets several applications be open at once; MacMusic plays on behind them.

Now Playing

The screen showing the song that’s playing.

Page Setup

The command, in the File menu, that sets the paper and which way up it goes, for the printer chosen in the Chooser.

playlist

A list of songs from your library, in the order you choose.

pointer

The arrow on the screen that moves as you move the mouse.

rating

From one to five stars, given to a song on Now Playing.

Repeat

A setting that plays one song, or a whole list, again and again.

rescan

To have MacMusic read its library’s folder again.

SCSI

Small Computer System Interface: the port on the back of the Macintosh Plus that a hard disk connects to.

scroll bar

A bar at the side of a list that shows there’s more than fits on the screen.

scrubber

A diamond on Now Playing’s progress bar, moved with the click wheel to go to any place in a song.

Shuffle

A setting that plays songs, or albums, in a random order.

song

One piece of music in your library, with its details: title, artist, album and so on.

song file

The file holding a song in your library: its WAV file, with MacMusic’s details added.

sound output port

The socket on the back of the Macintosh for headphones, a speaker or a tape recorder.

startup disk

The disk holding the System Folder the Macintosh started from.

track number

A song’s place on its album; an album’s songs are listed in track order.

WAV file

A kind of sound file. MacMusic plays WAV files that are mono, with 22,254 or 22,050 8-bit samples a second.

window

An area of the screen with a title bar, that shows a document, a disk’s contents or, in MacMusic’s case, the player.
