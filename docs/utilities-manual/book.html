About This Manual

MacMusic plays music on a Macintosh Plus. The MacMusic utilities are three programs for your Mac, a modern Mac, that look after that music: they make your songs into MacMusic’s sound files, take them to the Plus, and keep a safe copy of what’s there. This manual shows you how.

  • The MacMusic Converter Utility makes a song, from a sound file or a YouTube video, into a MacMusic WAV file, and an MP3 if you like.
  • The MacMusic Transfer Utility puts MacMusic WAV files on a disk for the Plus: a disk image that the Plus’s BlueSCSI reads from its SD card.
  • The Macintosh Backup Utility keeps copies of the BlueSCSI’s SD cards on your Mac, rather as Time Machine does for your Mac, so a disk can be put back if something on the Plus is lost.

What’s on the USB drive

The USB drive in the box holds:

  • the three utilities;
  • this manual and the MacMusic User’s Guide, to read on your Mac;
  • the MacMusic disk, for the Macintosh Plus;
  • the utilities’ source code and licences;
  • a Read Me.

CHECK: the drive’s contents are settled when it’s made (UB8).

What you need

  • a Mac with Apple silicon and macOS 26;
  • a Macintosh Plus with System 6.0.7 and MacMusic 1.0;
  • a BlueSCSI in the Plus, and a way to read its SD card on your Mac;
  • for YouTube, an internet connection.

Other MacMusic manuals

The MacMusic User’s Guide explains MacMusic itself: importing the songs this manual helps you make, and playing them. This manual names it wherever you’ll need it.

What you should know

This manual assumes you know your Mac: opening programs, using the Finder and its folders, and copying files. It also assumes you know the Macintosh Plus, as the MacMusic User’s Guide and the Macintosh Owner’s Guide teach it.

Two computers

The utilities run on one computer, and their work ends up on another. This manual always says which: your Mac is the modern one; the Macintosh Plus, or the Plus, is the one that plays MacMusic.

How to use this manual

  • Start here: Chapter 1, “Getting Started,” explains what’s on the USB drive, installs the utilities, and follows one song from your Mac to the Plus.
  • Each program: Chapters 2 to 4 take one utility each: “Converting Songs,” “Moving Songs to the Macintosh” and “Backing Up Your Macintosh.” Each has a lesson or two, the tasks it does one at a time, and a reference to every part of its window.

Introduction

The MacMusic Converter Utility makes your music into files that MacMusic can play. MacMusic plays one kind of sound file only: a WAV file that is mono, with 22,050 samples a second, 8 bits each (MacMusic User’s Guide, Appendix B). The Converter makes exactly that kind, from almost any sound file on your Mac, or from a YouTube video you own or have permission to save.

It runs on your Mac, not on the Macintosh Plus. Chapter 3 explains how to take what it makes to the Plus.

What it makes

Each time you convert a song, the Converter makes either or both of these:

  • A MacMusic WAV: mono, 22,050 Hz, 8-bit. It’s checked against MacMusic’s own rules before it’s kept, and it carries the song’s title, so MacMusic fills the title in for you when you import it.
  • An MP3: 192 kbps, stereo if the song is. It’s for listening on your Mac or elsewhere; it never goes to the Macintosh Plus.

Where songs come from

  • A sound file on your Mac: an MP3, AAC or M4A, WAV, FLAC, Opus and most other kinds.
  • A YouTube video: one at a time, by its address. The Converter doesn’t sign in to YouTube, so private, age-restricted and members-only videos can’t be saved. Use it only for videos you own or have permission to save.

Safe with your files

The Converter never changes the song you start from. It asks before it replaces anything. If you cancel, or quit while it works, nothing half-made is left behind, and every file is checked before it’s kept.

What it makes goes in a folder called MacMusic Converter, inside the Music folder in your home folder, unless you choose another.

1Converting a Song From a File

In this lesson you make a MacMusic WAV from an MP3 on your Mac. Any song you have will do; the pictures show “Good Luck, Babe!”.

Open the MacMusic Converter Utility.

It’s in the Applications folder; double-click its icon. Its window opens with Local File chosen at the top.

Click Choose File.

A dialog box opens, showing the folders on your Mac.

Find the song, select it, and click Open.

The window shows the song’s name and folder, and under them what it is: an MP3, at 44,100 samples a second, in stereo, three minutes 38 seconds long.

You can drag a song from the Finder onto the window instead.

Under Output, click MacMusic WAV.

The Title is filled in from the song’s own details, if it has any. This is the title MacMusic will show; type another if you like.

Leave File name empty, and the file is named after the title. Saves as shows the name it will have: Good Luck, Babe!.wav.

Click Convert.

At the foot of the window, the Converter says what it’s doing as it works: checking the file, analysing the sound, making the MacMusic WAV, and checking the result. A song of a few minutes takes a few seconds.

When it’s finished, Results shows the new file, its length, size and loudest peak, with a Show in Finder button to find it, and what was done to the sound. (The picture shows another song’s.)

2Converting a YouTube Video

Now you make a MacMusic WAV and an MP3 from a YouTube video. Use one you own or have permission to save; the pictures show a piece of music its channel offers free of copyright.

Click YouTube at the top of the window.

In your web browser, copy the video’s address. Then click in the box under YouTube Video, and choose Paste from the Edit menu.

Click Look Up.

The Converter asks YouTube about the video.

In a moment you see its title, its channel and its length.

If the address is of a video in a playlist, the playlist is ignored: only the one video is converted.

Under Output, click Both.

Type a shorter title: Chill Dreamy.

The title of a video is often long. MacMusic shows up to 63 characters of a title, and the WAV file’s name can be no more than 31, so a short title is best. Saves as shows the two files you’ll get.

Click Convert.

The Converter fetches the sound from YouTube first, then makes each file in turn.

Results shows what was done, and the files made. Click Show in Finder to see them in their folder.

This video was normalised by −2.4 dB, and its last three seconds of silence trimmed: those are choices under Sound, described in the tasks that follow.

If a Video Can’t Be Used

The Converter says why, in place of the video’s details:

  • The video is private.
  • YouTube needs signing in to play it (it may be age-restricted or for members only). The Converter doesn’t sign in.
  • It isn’t available in your country.
  • It isn’t available: it may have been removed, or the address is wrong.

  • WAV Only makes just the MacMusic WAV.
  • Cancel makes nothing.

Naming the Song

Each song has a title, which MacMusic shows, and a file name, which is what the file is called on your Mac and on the Macintosh Plus.

To Set the Title MacMusic Shows

Type it in the Title box.

It’s filled in for you from the song’s own details, or the video’s title. It goes inside the WAV file, and MacMusic puts it in the Title box when you import the song. MacMusic keeps up to 63 characters.

To Set the File Name

Type it in the File name box, without .wav or .mp3.

Left empty, the file is named after the title.

A MacMusic WAV’s name can be no more than 31 characters, with .wav (the Macintosh’s limit), so a long name is cut, at a word where it can be. Colons are left out, and letters the Macintosh can’t show are changed for the nearest it can. An MP3 keeps its whole name.

Saves as shows the names before you convert.

The Sound

Two choices under Sound change the music a little, and both start off. The Converter remembers them for next time.

To Even Out the Loudness

Turn on Normalise.

The song is made louder or quieter so that its loudest moment comes to a set level (−1.0 dBFS, just below the most a file can hold). Songs from different places then play at more nearly the same volume.

It sets the loudest peak, not how loud a song sounds overall, so songs may still sound different.

To Trim Silence at the Ends

Turn on “Trim silence at the ends,” and choose 2 or 3 seconds in Longer than.

Silence longer than that at the start or the end is cut down to half a second. Silence in the middle of a song is never touched.

To Change the Normalise Level

Click Advanced, near the foot of the window, to open it.

Click the arrows beside Normalise to.

The level goes from −6.0 to 0.0 dBFS, half a step at a time. The window’s description of Normalise changes to match.

Saving

To Choose Where Files Are Saved

To go back to the MacMusic Converter folder in your Music folder, click Use Default. That folder is made the first time it’s needed.

If a File Is Already There

  • Keep Both saves the new file under the next free name: Chill Dreamy 2.wav, say.
  • Replace puts the new file in place of the old one.
  • Cancel makes nothing.

The song you’re converting from is never replaced: if it has the same name, the new file takes the next free one.

Click Cancel, or press Esc.

It stops, and nothing half-made is kept: Results says so.

  • Keep Converting carries on.
  • Stop and Quit stops it and quits. The file being made isn’t saved; anything already finished is kept.

Afterwards

Results, at the foot of the window, shows what was made.

Introduction

The MacMusic Transfer Utility takes your MacMusic WAV files to the Macintosh Plus. It puts them on a disk the Plus can use: a disk image, which the BlueSCSI in the Plus reads from its SD card as though it were another hard disk.

You copy the disk image onto the card yourself, with the Finder; the Transfer Utility never writes to the card.

What it makes

A disk called Transfer, holding a folder called WAV Files with your songs in it, kept in one file on your Mac, such as HD60_512 Transfer.hda. Its name tells the BlueSCSI what it is: a hard disk at SCSI ID 6 (Appendix B, “Disk Images”). It’s made the size the songs need, with a quarter as much room again for more.

SCSI IDs

Each disk on the Plus has a SCSI ID, 0 to 6 (7 is the Macintosh itself), and each disk image on the card needs an ID of its own. Your startup disk is usually 0. The Transfer Utility suggests 6.

Safe with your files

It checks every song against MacMusic’s own rules first, and refuses any that aren’t MacMusic WAV files; it never changes a song; and it checks the finished disk, file by file, before it keeps it. Nothing half-made is left behind.

1Putting Songs on a Disk for the Plus

In this lesson you put songs made by the Converter on a disk image, copy it to the card, and find the songs on the Plus.

Open the MacMusic Transfer Utility.

Its window waits for songs.

Drag MacMusic WAV files onto the window, or a folder of them, or click Add Files.

Each song is listed with its name on the Mac, its format, length and size, and a tick if it can go on the disk.

Under Disk, the SCSI ID is 6, and the file name and size of the disk image are shown.

Click Build Transfer Image.

The Transfer Utility makes the disk, copies the songs onto it, and checks it, saying at the foot of the window what it’s doing.

When it’s done, Result shows the disk image, and what to do with it.

Click Show in Finder.

Shut the Plus down with Shut Down, switch it off, take the card out of the BlueSCSI, and put it in your Mac.

Drag the disk image onto the card, into its top level, not into a folder; then eject the card.

Put the card back in the BlueSCSI, and switch the Plus on.

A disk called Transfer appears on the Plus’s desktop, with your songs in its WAV Files folder.

Open the Transfer disk, and its WAV Files folder.

The songs show most clearly by name: choose by Name from the View menu.

Import them with MacMusic’s Import Song: see the MacMusic User’s Guide, Lesson 7.

2Adding More Songs Later

Later you’ll have more songs for the Plus. There are two ways.

  • On the Plus: the Transfer disk has room to spare (a quarter again), so songs can be copied onto it there, from a floppy disk, say.
  • A new disk image: add all the songs, old and new, to the Transfer Utility, and build again. It asks what to do with the image already in the folder: Replace it, or Keep Both.

To replace the disk on the card, drag the new image onto the card as before; the Finder asks whether to replace the old one, which has the same name.

MacMusic’s library on the Plus is separate from the Transfer disk: a song imported into it stays there, whatever becomes of the disk.

Choosing the Songs

Only MacMusic WAV files can go on the disk: those the Converter makes.

To Add Songs

Drag WAV files, or a folder of them, onto the window.

OR

Click Add Files, choose them, and click Open.

A folder adds the WAV files in it, not those in folders inside it. The same file added twice is listed once.

To Remove Songs

Select them in the list and click Remove; or click Clear All.

The files themselves aren’t touched.

If a Song Can’t Be Used

Its Status says Can’t be used; point to it to see why: it isn’t a WAV file, or isn’t in MacMusic’s format (mono, 22,050 Hz, 8-bit).

Remove it, or make a MacMusic WAV of it with the Converter, and add that instead.

The disk isn’t built while a refused song is in the list.

If a Name Is Changed

A Macintosh file name is at most 31 characters, without colons, and two names that differ only in capitals count as the same. Where a name has to change, the new one is shown in orange under On the Mac: a long name shortened, a colon made a hyphen, a second “Clash.wav” beside “clash.wav” made “Clash 2.wav,” “Rock: Live.wav” made “Rock- Live.wav.” The file on your Mac keeps its name.

The Disk

The disk’s SCSI ID, its name and its size are under Disk.

To Choose the SCSI ID

Choose an ID from the SCSI ID menu: 0 to 6.

The file name changes to match: HD50_512 Transfer.hda for 5. Choose an ID no other disk image on the card uses.

ID 0 is usually your startup disk’s: if you choose it, you’re asked to confirm (Use ID 0 Anyway), since putting the image on the card would replace your startup disk.

How the Size Is Worked Out

The songs’ size, plus a quarter as much again for adding more, plus 4 MiB for the disk’s own needs, rounded up to whole megabytes; at least 10 MiB, and at most 2 GB, the largest disk System 6 can use. Click “How is this worked out?” under Size to see the sums.

Sizes are shown as the Finder shows them (46.1 MB). On the Plus, sizes are in K of 1,024 bytes, so the same disk shows there as about 45,000K.

  • Replace puts the new one in place of the old.
  • Cancel builds nothing.

Introduction

Everything on your Macintosh Plus lives on the BlueSCSI’s SD card: its disks, with the System, your programs, and MacMusic and its songs. Cards wear out, and a power cut while the Plus is using the card can damage a disk on it. The Macintosh Backup Utility keeps copies of the card on your Mac, so that whatever happens to the card, its disks can be put back as they were.

It works rather as Time Machine does for your Mac: each backup is kept, dated, and older ones are thinned out as time goes on.

Snapshots

Each backup is a snapshot: a complete copy of everything on the card at that moment, in a folder of its own on your Mac, with a record (its manifest) of every file and a check-sum of each. A snapshot stands on its own: you can restore from it even if every other snapshot were lost.

Safe with your card

The Backup Utility never writes to the card it backs up. It reads every file, copies it, and checks the copy against what it read; only a backup in which every file checks becomes a snapshot. It never deletes a backup unless you ask, or under the thinning rule you can turn off.

Where the backups go

In a folder called Mac Plus Backups, in your home folder, unless you choose another in Settings. A card of 2 GB of disks takes about 2 GB for each snapshot, so keep an eye on the room on your Mac.

Older Snapshots and Settings

After each backup, older snapshots of the card are thinned out, so the backups don’t fill your Mac.

How Older Snapshots Are Thinned

For each card, the Backup Utility keeps:

  • the newest snapshot of each day, for a week;
  • the newest of each week, for a month;
  • the newest of each month, for a year.

It always keeps at least two, and never one marked Keep. Where it can, it keeps one whose disks were all shut down cleanly. Those removed go to the Trash, and the window says which.

Appendix B

About the Files

MacMusic WAV Files

MacMusic plays one kind of sound file only: a WAV file of plain sound (“PCM”), mono, with 22,050 samples a second, each of 8 bits. The MacMusic User’s Guide, Appendix B, explains why. The Converter makes exactly this kind, and puts the song’s title inside it, where MacMusic finds it when you import the song.

A MacMusic WAV takes 22,050 bytes for each second of sound:

Holds
A minute of musicabout 1.3 MB
A 3½-minute songabout 4.6 MB
An 800K floppy diskabout 37 seconds
A 20 MB hard diskabout 15 minutes
A 100 MB diskabout 75 minutes

Its name is at most 31 characters, with .wav, since that’s the longest a Macintosh file name can be.

MP3 Files

The Converter’s MP3s are 192 kbps, stereo if the song is: about 1.4 MB a minute. They’re for your Mac, a phone or any other player; MacMusic doesn’t play them. An MP3’s name keeps every character of the title.

Disk Images

A disk image is a whole hard disk kept in one file. A BlueSCSI, fitted to the Macintosh Plus’s SCSI port, reads disk images from its SD card, and the Plus sees each as a hard disk of its own.

The BlueSCSI reads what a disk image is from its name:

Part of the nameMeans
HDa hard disk
6its SCSI ID, 0 to 6 (7 is the Macintosh itself)
0the first (and only) disk at that ID
_512blocks of 512 bytes, the Plus’s own
Transfera label, for you
.hdaa hard disk image

So HD60_512 Transfer.hda is a hard disk at SCSI ID 6. Each image on the card needs an ID of its own.

A disk made by the Transfer Utility starts as Apple’s hard disks do, with a partition map and Apple’s hard disk driver (from its disk template), and holds one disk, called Transfer, with one folder, WAV Files, holding the songs. Its size is the songs’, plus a quarter as much again for adding more, plus 4 MB for the disk’s own needs, rounded up to a whole megabyte: at least 10 MB, and at most 2 GB, the largest disk System 6 can use.

CHECK: confirmed on the Macintosh Plus with the Transfer Utility’s first disk (2026-10-09); its 1.0 check is pending.

Names on the Macintosh

Macintosh file names are at most 31 characters, can’t contain a colon, and ignore the difference between capital and small letters. So the Transfer Utility changes names where it must: a long name is shortened; a colon becomes a hyphen (Rock: Live.wav becomes Rock- Live.wav); and of two names that differ only in capitals, the second gets a number (Clash.wav, beside clash.wav, becomes Clash 2.wav). Letters the Macintosh can’t show are changed for the nearest it can. The window shows every new name before the disk is made.

The Backup Folder

The Macintosh Backup Utility keeps everything in one folder, Mac Plus Backups in your home folder unless you choose another:

Snapshotsa folder for each snapshot, named by its date and time and a number (2026-10-10_12-05-13_4914, say), holding Files, a copy of everything on the card, and SnapshotManifest.json, the record of every file with its size and check-sum (SHA-256)
Catalogthe list of snapshots, for quick browsing (catalog.json); the registered cards (cards.json); the snapshots marked Keep (pins.json)
Logsmade with the folder; 1.0 keeps nothing in it

A snapshot is never changed once it’s made, and each stands on its own: its disk images are ordinary files, which can be copied straight back onto a card with the Finder. A backup still in progress is in a hidden folder, and only gets its name when every file has been checked. Leave the folder’s contents to the Backup Utility: don’t rename or change them.

Each snapshot takes as much room as the card’s files: a card with 2 GB of disks takes about 2 GB a backup. Thinning keeps that in check.

Appendix C

Troubleshooting

On Your Mac

A program won’t open the first time

The utilities are made for your own use, and aren’t registered with Apple, so macOS may refuse to open one, saying it can’t check it. If it does, open System Settings, click Privacy & Security, and click Open Anyway beside the program’s name; then open it again. After that it opens normally. (A program copied from the USB drive usually opens straight away.)

CHECK: not yet seen with these programs on macOS 26.

YouTube stops working

If the Converter says YouTube has changed in a way its yt-dlp can’t handle, the Converter needs a newer yt-dlp inside it. That means rebuilding the Converter from its source, as its BUILDING.md describes (tools/update-yt-dlp.sh). Converting files from your Mac still works meanwhile.

Songs aren’t equally loud

Normalise evens out each song’s loudest moment, not how loud it sounds overall, so a quiet song with one loud drum may still sound quieter than others. MacMusic’s own volume sets the level you hear.

A file name has changed

A MacMusic WAV’s name is at most 31 characters, with no colons: see Appendix B, “Names on the Macintosh.” Type a shorter File name in the Converter for a better one.

Backups

The card isn’t recognised

The Backup Utility knows a BlueSCSI card by its bluescsi.ini, its log, or its disk images. If it doesn’t recognise one, click Choose Card and choose it yourself.

CHECK: not yet seen with a real card in this Mac.

The card can’t be read

The first time, macOS asks whether the Backup Utility may read removable disks. If you said no, allow it in System Settings, Privacy & Security, Files & Folders, and choose the card again.

The backup says the card changed

Something else read or wrote the card during the backup: the Finder making its hidden files, perhaps, or another program. Back up again.

The backups take too much room

Turn on thinning in Settings, delete snapshots you don’t need, or choose a backup folder on a bigger disk. Deleted snapshots go to the Trash: empty it to get the room back.

A disk on the Plus has gone wrong

Restore its disk image from a snapshot from before it went wrong (Chapter 4, Lesson 2). A snapshot marked with a warning sign has a disk that wasn’t shut down cleanly; one before it may be better.

The card itself has failed

Restore the newest snapshot onto a new card (Chapter 4, “To Restore Onto a New Card”).

On the Macintosh Plus

The disk doesn’t appear

Check that the image’s name starts HD and a SCSI ID (0 to 6), that no other image on the card has the same ID, and that the card went back into the BlueSCSI before you switched the Plus on.

CHECK: to be completed after the Transfer Utility’s Plus check.

A song won’t import

MacMusic says why: see Appendix A of the MacMusic User’s Guide. A file made by the Converter, or put on a disk by the Transfer Utility, has already been checked against MacMusic’s rules, so the commonest cause is a file that came another way.

Glossary

Apple silicon

The kind of processor in Macs made since 2020. The utilities need one.

backup folder

The folder on your Mac where the Backup Utility keeps its snapshots: Mac Plus Backups, in your home folder, unless you choose another.

BlueSCSI

A small board that plugs into the Macintosh Plus’s SCSI port and acts as one or more hard disks, reading disk images from an SD card.

check-sum

A number worked out from every byte of a file, so that a copy can be checked against it: one byte different, and the number is different. The Backup Utility uses SHA-256.

clean shutdown

A disk put away properly, as Shut Down does. A disk that lost power while the Plus was using it wasn’t shut down cleanly, and may be damaged.

dBFS

Decibels below full scale: how loud a sound is compared with the loudest a file can hold. 0 dBFS is the most; −1.0 dBFS is just below it.

disk image

A whole disk kept in one file. The Transfer Utility makes them; a BlueSCSI reads them.

disk template

The start of a BlueSCSI disk image (its partition map and Apple’s hard disk driver), which the Transfer Utility copies at the start of every disk it makes.

eject

To tell your Mac you’ve finished with a disk or card, so it can be taken out safely.

exFAT

The way of keeping files that SD cards for a BlueSCSI use, which your Mac reads and writes.

Finder

The program that shows your files, folders and disks, on your Mac and on the Macintosh Plus alike.

HFS

The Macintosh’s own way of keeping files on a disk, which System 6 uses. A Transfer Utility disk is an HFS disk.

import

In MacMusic, to add a song to your library, with Import Song from the File menu (MacMusic User’s Guide).

incomplete job

A backup that didn’t finish, or wasn’t verified. It holds no snapshot, and can be cleared.

kbps

Thousands of bits a second: how much an MP3 uses for each second of sound. The Converter’s MP3s are 192 kbps.

MacMusic WAV

The one kind of sound file MacMusic plays: a WAV file, mono, with 22,050 samples a second, 8 bits each. The Converter makes them.

manifest

A snapshot’s record of every file in it, with its size and check-sum.

Mini vMac

A program that makes your Mac act as a Macintosh Plus. Some of this manual’s pictures of the Plus come from it.

mono

Sound on one channel. MacMusic plays in mono, since the Macintosh has one sound channel; a stereo song is mixed into one.

MP3

A kind of sound file that takes much less room than a WAV file, for listening on your Mac or elsewhere. MacMusic doesn’t play them.

normalise

To make a song louder or quieter, so that its loudest moment reaches a set level.

peak

A song’s loudest moment.

re-encode

To make an MP3 from a song that’s already an MP3 (or another compressed kind), which loses a little quality.

register

In the Backup Utility, to give a card a name, so it’s recognised whenever it’s inserted and its backups are kept together.

restore

To copy files back out of a snapshot.

Results

The part of the Converter’s window that shows what a conversion made.

sample rate

How many times a second a sound is measured: 22,050 for a MacMusic WAV, 44,100 for a CD.

Saves as

In the Converter, the names the files will have, shown before you convert.

SCSI ID

A number, 0 to 7, that tells apart the devices on the Macintosh Plus’s SCSI port. 7 is the Macintosh itself; a startup disk is usually 0. Each disk image on a BlueSCSI’s card has one.

SD card

The small memory card a BlueSCSI keeps its disk images on. You copy disk images to it on your Mac.

snapshot

A complete copy of everything on a card at one moment, made by the Backup Utility and checked file by file.

thinning

Removing older snapshots after a backup, so that the backups don’t fill your Mac: one a day for a week, one a week for a month, one a month for a year.

title

A song’s name as MacMusic shows it. The Converter puts it inside the WAV file.

Transfer disk

The disk the Transfer Utility makes, called Transfer, with your songs in its WAV Files folder.

trim silence

In the Converter, to cut long silence at the start or end of a song down to half a second.

verify

To check every file of a snapshot against its manifest.

WAV file

A common kind of sound file. MacMusic plays one kind of WAV file only: see MacMusic WAV.

YouTube address

A video’s web address, from your browser’s address bar, such as https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=….
